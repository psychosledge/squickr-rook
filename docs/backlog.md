- #14 feature, online: The host controls seating in the online room lobby, and players no longer claim their own seats. Each joining player is auto-seated in a random open seat, and once all four seats are full later joiners wait unseated. - #16 feature, bot: Bots bid more aggressively and weigh score risk against reward. Today a human can buy the bid cheap and make it on a junk hand, and bots rarely go above 135. At a human table most winning bids are 160 to 170, 150 is rare, and below 150 almost never happens. Aggression scales by difficulty, Beginner soft to Expert aggressive. Bids weigh what going set costs against what letting the opponents have an easier shot at winning costs: push harder as the opponents near 500, both to take the bid and to drive their price up without intending to win. Moon awareness carries into ordinary bids: avoid a set that drops the team below 0, since a moon made from 0 or above wins outright while one made from below 0 only resets the score to 0, so a moon from -10 gains little and one from -400 is worth the risk. In packages/engine/src/bot.ts, baseBidCeiling maps a strong hand to 130 to 150, and score context in computeBidCeiling only adds 8 or 15 when trailing by 100 or 200. Tuning target and make rate get settled through simulator runs, checking the distribution of winning bids by difficulty (2026-11-20)
- #17 feature, bot: Bots follow partner bidding etiquette. Opening at 100 keeps options open and works as a defensive signal. If the partner then passes, the opener may still push a weak hand to defend; if the partner bids, the opener reads it as a hand strong enough to play offensively and backs off. Partners generally bid against each other no more than once, especially when they are the only bidders left. Today partner bid inference in computeBidCeiling raises a bot's ceiling when its partner has bid, which makes outbidding the partner more likely. Related to #16 (2026-11-20)
- #18 feature, bot: Bots don't overtake a trick their partner already has won, whether by trumping or by playing a higher card of the led suit. They play a weak card instead and keep strong cards for later. Seen in a live Expert game: the bot won the bid and, out of the led suit, trumped a trick the human partner had already caught, when sluffing a weak off-suit card would have saved the trump. Applies to any seat. Overtaking on purpose to take the lead is a separate decision and out of scope (2026-11-20)
- #19 feature, bot: Trump management once a team holds all remaining trump. Each trump is then a sure winner, so a bot out of the led suit trumps in when an opponent leads and points are in play, unless its partner already has the trick (#18). The team also stops leading trump: leading trump after the partner bid is standard while opponents still hold trump, but once they hold none it only spends sure winners. Seen in live Expert games, where the bidder's partner saved trump instead of catching points (2026-11-20)
- #20 feature, bot tooling: Export an offline game's event log from the web app, including production builds, and load it in /replay, so a bot mistake seen in a real game can be reviewed and cited as a game, trick, and perspective. The offline store already keeps the full eventLog from GameStarted with its seed, and bot decisions are seeded. Format and where the export control lives get settled in /story (2026-11-20)
- #21 feature, bot: Advanced lead control. A bot overtakes a trick its partner already has won when it needs the lead to use its hand. The motivating case: the bidder holds off-suit cards that are now sure winners, since every higher card in the suit has been played, but they only win if that suit is led, and the partner may not lead it. Taking the lead lets the bidder cash those cards as if they were trump, then finish with the remaining trump. Needs played-card tracking to spot sure winners, which Expert bots already do. Exception to #18, likely Hard and Expert only (2026-11-20)
